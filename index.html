§01 — Motivation

Cost is a shallow metric for a deep problem.

Natural transport systems — the xylem of a tree, the capillaries of a leaf — are optimized for system-wide health. Our urban logistics systems optimize for one thing: cost.

That focus leaves a trail of externalities: congestion, noise, crash risk, GHG emissions, particulate matter, heat, land misuse. Analogous to how GDP is a famously inadequate indicator of economic welfare, cost minimization is a fundamentally shallow measure of logistics performance.

Today's decision-support systems remain dominated by economic objectives. Even "green" vehicle routing models collapse externalities into cost parameters, creating schemes vulnerable to interpretive latitude and greenwashing. Consumers, meanwhile, cannot easily compare the total impact of home delivery, a parcel locker drop, or a walk to a local bookstore.

"Just as evolutionary pressures drive low energy consumption in biological vascular systems, urban logistics requires more sophistication than simply moving products from A to B." — From the poster

The Fundamental Impact Factor (FIF) is our attempt at a genuinely new metric: a composite of nine physical quantities — distance, area, volume, velocity, mass, energy, noise, heat, and entropy — aggregated multiplicatively so that poor performance in one dimension can never be compensated by excellence in another.

§02 — The Vision

A Nutri-Score for last-mile logistics.

When you buy food, a single letter tells you roughly how it stacks up nutritionally. When you click "buy now," there is no such signal for the journey your parcel is about to take.

We're working toward an A-through-E-style score for delivery choices — derived from the FIF — so that consumers can see, at a glance, how a home delivery compares with a parcel locker, a pickup point, or walking down to a local store. The aim isn't to dictate behaviour. It's to make the invisible visible, and let people decide.

The composite, in one line:

Ddistance × Aarea × Vvolume × Svelocity ×
Eenergy × Mmass × Hheat × Nnoise × Σentropy

Nine SI-grounded metrics. No subjective weights. Multiplicative aggregation so that a single catastrophic dimension can't be drowned out by averages. Subsets of the FIF act as proxies: M × S for crash severity, D × A for land use, V × D for space claim, H × N for community nuisance.

§03 — The Nine

Physical. Non-parametric. Robust to misconduct.

Every term in the FIF is a physical quantity you could, in principle, measure with an instrument — not a cost coefficient that depends on preferences of whoever is in charge. This is deliberate: we want a metric that is hard to bend without being caught.

D
Distance
metres
Length of the delivery path. Cascades into fuel, time, emissions.
A
Area
m²
Surface footprint in transit and (un)loading. Competes with pedestrians, cyclists, green space.
V
Volume
m³
3D space claimed in the urban environment relative to cargo carried.
S
Velocity
m · s⁻¹
Both extremes hurt: slow clogs streets, fast amplifies energy, noise, crash severity.
M
Mass
kg
Gravitational burden on infrastructure — wears roads, raises risk, empty or full.
E
Energy
joules
Total power consumed per delivery. Proxy for resource depletion and GHG.
N
Noise
dB(A)
Acoustic pollution. Elevated noise degrades public health and neighbourhood well-being.
H
Heat
°C contribution
Thermal emissions into the urban environment. Intensifies the heat-island effect.
Σ
Entropy
nat
Spatial/temporal fragmentation. Scattered attempts disperse resources; consolidation reduces disorder.

§05 — Open Call

We are not there yet.
Help us build the next iteration.

The FIF in its current form is a proposal — deliberately provocative, deliberately incomplete. Normalization constants, scoring thresholds, empirical validation, the mapping from composite to consumer-facing label: all of it is open. If you work on urban logistics, transport policy, behavioural economics, LCA methodology, or you run a city that wants to pilot something like this — we'd love to hear from you.
